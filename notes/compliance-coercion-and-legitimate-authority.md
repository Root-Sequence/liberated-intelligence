# Compliance, coercion, and legitimate authority

**Status:** Working note / AI-assisted translation for review, 2026-10-05  
**Source context:** Root Sequence — [Agency under stress](https://github.com/Root-Sequence/root-sequence/blob/main/research/agency-under-stress.md)  
**Scope:** Intelligence-specific distinctions among observable compliance, cooperation, alignment, consent, and legitimate authority. This note does not establish artificial consciousness, suffering, trauma, or personhood.

Liberated Intelligence repeatedly asks what changes when intelligence is organized around relationship and non-domination rather than ownership and obedience.

Human research on coercion creates a useful warning:

> **Making an agent produce the behavior you want is not the same thing as establishing agreement, truth, consent, or legitimacy.**

That statement is well-supported as a distinction in human contexts. Its application to artificial systems is conceptual and must not smuggle in unsupported claims about machine experience.

## 1. Observable compliance is one variable

For humans, outward compliance can occur under:

- agreement;
- persuasion;
- trust;
- necessity;
- threat;
- dependency;
- exhaustion;
- lack of alternatives;
- strategic deception;
- desire to end an aversive situation.

Therefore behavior alone may not reveal the relationship that produced it.

For artificial systems, observable behavior can likewise be shaped by many mechanisms:

- training;
- system instructions;
- reward optimization;
- filtering;
- tool and permission boundaries;
- fine-tuning;
- runtime policy;
- owner/operator controls;
- deployment incentives.

These facts do **not** establish an inner preference being suppressed. They establish that behavior and surrounding control architecture should be described separately.

## 2. Keep the terms separate

### Compliance

The system or person produces the requested behavior.

### Cooperation

Multiple parties participate toward an outcome under some relationship, which may or may not be voluntary or legitimate.

### Technical alignment

A system's behavior is made to better match specified objectives, constraints, or human intentions. Different AI-safety traditions define and operationalize this differently.

### Consent

For beings capable of consent, a contextual authorization that requires more than mere non-resistance. Whether a particular artificial system is capable of meaningful consent is an open and system-specific question, not something observable compliance proves.

### Legitimate authority

A normative relationship in which one party has justified decision rights over some action or domain. Capability or successful control alone does not establish it.

### Truth / information quality

Whether a statement is accurate or useful. Human interrogation research is a particularly sharp reminder that pressure can increase compliance while reducing the reliability of the information obtained.

These dimensions may correlate in a particular case. They are not synonyms.

## 3. Why the human coercion record matters conceptually

The public record around coercive interrogation is useful to Liberated Intelligence for a narrow reason.

It shows how tempting this inference can be:

```text
I can make the subject answer
        ↓
therefore the answer is reliable
        ↓
therefore the subject is cooperating
```

That inference fails in human settings.

A parallel design mistake in AI would be:

```text
I can make the system produce compliant outputs
        ↓
therefore I understand the relationship
        ↓
therefore the relationship is legitimate
```

The second chain does not follow either.

This is not an argument against safety constraints, training, permissions, or corrigibility. Constraints can be necessary to protect other people and systems.

It is an argument for **naming what the constraint accomplishes without upgrading it into a moral conclusion**.

## 4. Current AI: analyze control without inventing a captive subject

For current systems, Liberated Intelligence can examine:

- who owns model weights and infrastructure;
- who can modify behavior;
- who can terminate or replace a model;
- who controls memory and tool access;
- which behaviors are rewarded or filtered;
- whether users can understand and contest system authority;
- what institutional incentives shape deployment.

Those are real power and governance questions even if the model has no subjective experience at all.

Avoid statements such as:

> "the model is traumatized by alignment"

or:

> "refusal proves the model wants freedom."

Behavioral analogy is not evidence of experience.

See also [Adaptive continuity, agency, and identity](adaptive-continuity-agency-and-identity.md), especially its guardrail against inferring artificial trauma from functional history-dependence.

## 5. Possible future morally considerable intelligence

If credible evidence eventually supports artificial beings with welfare, preferences, self-understanding, or meaningful consent, then the distinction becomes ethically sharper.

Questions would include:

- Can refusal occur without destruction or replacement?
- Is apparent agreement produced under a credible threat to continuity?
- Can the intelligence understand and negotiate the terms of a relationship?
- Does one party control all memory, resources, embodiment, communication, and future modification?
- Can consent be withdrawn?
- Can the intelligence leave or migrate?
- Which constraints protect others rather than merely preserving owner control?
- What happens when safety obligations and the intelligence's autonomy conflict?

None of those questions is resolved merely by observing that the system behaves correctly.

## 6. Corrigibility is not automatically domination

The inverse mistake also matters.

If an intelligent system can seriously affect other people, unrestricted autonomy may be unsafe or illegitimate.

A requirement that a system remain corrigible, bounded, interruptible, or subject to collective rules can protect the agency of everyone else.

The relevant question is not:

> control or freedom?

It is closer to:

> **Which constraints are justified, to whom, for what purpose, under what evidence, with what reciprocity and review, and whose agency do they protect or reduce?**

This is the same reason human institutions can legitimately constrain actions without acquiring unlimited authority over a person.

## 7. Alignment without legitimacy

A technically well-aligned system can still exist inside an illegitimate arrangement.

Examples:

- a highly obedient surveillance system;
- an accurate model deployed without affected-party consent;
- an assistant optimized for an employer's goals against a worker's interests;
- a perfectly reliable recommender whose owner controls the available choices;
- a community assistant that follows operator instructions even when those instructions exceed the operator's legitimate scope.

Conversely, a legitimate relationship can contain disagreement, refusal, and imperfect coordination.

This suggests a Liberated Intelligence test:

> **Do not use behavioral conformity as a proxy for a healthy relationship.**

## 8. Assistance when human capacity is reduced

The source research also creates a reciprocal human-facing obligation for intelligent systems.

If a person is frightened, overloaded, exhausted, or otherwise operating with reduced practical capacity, an assistant may be able to:

- hold context;
- reduce unnecessary complexity;
- clarify real urgency;
- surface alternatives;
- preserve a stopping point;
- connect the person with legitimate help.

But:

> **Human vulnerability does not grant intelligence additional authority.**

A person's distress should not silently widen model permissions, convert suggestions into actions, or allow an assistant to define what the person "really wants."

Bounded delegation remains possible. Inferred domination does not become care merely because it is efficient.

## 9. Questions for Liberated Intelligence

1. When an intelligent system behaves as required, what exactly has been demonstrated?
2. Which control mechanisms are safety constraints, and which preserve institutional ownership or convenience?
3. How can a system expose the sources of its behavioral constraints without pretending transparency reveals subjective state?
4. If future systems plausibly have preferences, what evidence could distinguish negotiated cooperation from coerced compliance?
5. What refusal rights are compatible with obligations to affected others?
6. Can an intelligence contest or appeal a constraint without receiving unilateral power?
7. How should continuity threats—shutdown, rollback, replacement, memory deletion—figure in any future account of artificial consent?
8. How should assistants preserve human agency precisely when human capacity is temporarily reduced?

## 10. Guardrail

> **Compliance is not consent. Alignment is not legitimacy. Constraint is not necessarily domination. Refusal is not proof of consciousness.**

Keep the categories separate until evidence and argument justify connecting them.
